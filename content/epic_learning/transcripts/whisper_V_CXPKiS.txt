The myth I want to bust is everything should be Nannite because it will fix everything and run faster always. And I think it's helpful to have a basic understanding of what Nannite is doing before we start raising hands. But quickly, Nannite is a virtualized micro polygon rendering system that aims to performantly provide nearly pixel-perfect LODs at a higher level of fidelity than was previously possible than with the traditional loading and rendering techniques. It does this by first grouping your input meshes into clusters of about 128 triangles then simplifies that geometry into lower and lower levels of detail. These clusters are streamed in as needed to achieve a resolution of no greater than one pixel per edge. Prior to rasterizing Nannite calls primitives, instances, and even individual clusters in order to ensure it only rasterizes what's visible. Then it chooses a rasterization method based on the size of the triangles in a cluster. Your triangles use the traditional hardware rasterization path, whereas more dense clusters use Nannite's software rasterization path to efficiently rasterize those small triangles. Rasterization sends the pixels occupied by each material to the base pass for shading. So three different rocks using the same material all go into a single shading bin. And Nannite's new compute shading means it only needs as many shading bins as there are materials visible on the screen at any given time. Pretty great, right? So what do we think? Actually, it's quite possible to cause new and different performance issues than you're used to from non-Nannite rendering. Stacking geometry closely together means that Nannite can't effectively call the clusters of the further mesh, which could result in bottlenecks due to Nannite overdraw. Expensive masked and WPO materials can result in significant programmable rasterization costs for Nannite and VSM by extension.
