In this course, we'll be diving into everything you need to know in order to improve your lighting in Unreal Engine. We'll be lighting an exterior environment and character from scratch by harnessing the power that RayTracing gives us. Now before we get started, here are some hardware recommendations for the course. We recommend having an Nvidia 2000 series or higher. This is because RayTracing needs the RTX capabilities of Nvidia's more recent graphic cards. Any DXR capable GPU will work, but you may suffer from performance loss without RTX. While working on lower end graphic cards, the frame rate can suffer, but the process is still entirely doable with the engine scalability settings, as well as changing sample counts, which will be explained a little later. I want to take the time to clarify a few things. First off, this course is not for LED volume use. Lighting this way is meant for offline rendering with the movie render queue. There may be artifacting in a viewport at times, but having the freedom to change any and every light in the scene means that this workflow is great for making pivotal creative decisions with a near final result in real time. We aren't lighting this for in-game use, and that's important because of the style and approach we're going to take. Lighting from a cinematographer's point of view. We'll be starting off with a very fundamental by organizing our scenes and setting up persistent levels correctly for proper scene management. Next, we're going to explore what it means to achieve cinematic lighting by breaking down how cinematographers and gaffers light real world film sets. We're then going to dive into light actors, how they fit into a next-tier scenario, and next is where things get really exciting, and that's your first lighting setup. You're going to learn how to quickly create an atmosphere light setup with the help of the environment light mixer. Next, we'll learn how to adjust the exposure of our scene and create realistic environmental lighting effects using real world values with the help of the post-process volume, cine camera actor, fog, and global illumination. What we have a grasp of the basics will approach how to light our scene using an HDRI backdrop and local light to mimic an overcast day. Then we'll light a night time scene with a combination of natural and practical light sources and apply ray trace lighting onto a character within our scene. And last but not least, we'll use a variety of static meshes and lighting channel to emulate traditional grip equipment in order to help us shape and bend the light to achieve a very specific effect.
