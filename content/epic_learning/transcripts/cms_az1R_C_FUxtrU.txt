Now that we understand the challenges we faced while developing our motion matching solution and the additional requirements specific to Fortnite, let's dive into the recipe we deem necessary, starting with some fundamentals. So let's add some context and terminology we can reuse later on. Motion matching is an animation technique that automates the transitions between nodes of a motion graph, where the nodes are simple poses of animation from a database, and the selected transition is the one that scores the best given a matrix between two poses, where one of them is the query pose. In our implementation, the simplest setup consists in a character with a movement component running an Animation Blueprint with a motion matching node and a pose history node. The motion matching node will search a given database using a query constructed from historical information from the post history node and a motion model prediction from the movement component. If the search selects something other than the continuing pose, defined as the pose that comes as a continuation of the previously-selected animation asset, the motion matching node will perform a blend into the newly-selected animation asset, repeating these cycles every frame. With this understanding on how the matching is performed, let's introduce the database schema, the asset defining the metric between poses and specifying what we care to match-- for example, the character feet position and the future trajectory and how much we care about those features via their weight. We define the schema as a collection of channels, simple building blocks to match positions, velocities, headings, and phases, composable in more complex channels to match trajectories or entire poses. With the basics out of the way, let's continue our dive into the Fortnite motion matching recipe. Because of Fortnite's tight memory budget, we decided against dance cards. And instead, we started with well-crafted short animations with higher connectivities for those pose features we wanted to match, like character fit positions and velocities to facilitate the motion graphs automated transitions. We also organize our animations in multiple databases driven by gameplay logic to discern pivots from start stops, idles, and run cycles, as well as being able to differentiate high-end from low-end platform setups. But ultimately, we had little data that sometimes poorly matched our twitchy motion model. We knew we needed to construct motion matching queries that better reflects the manifold of our sample animation poses. So we started with manipulating the input trajectory generation by mapping motion model velocity and accelerations as well as doctoring of rotation rates and filtering out spamming input behaviors. For moving platforms, Icy Feet, and Hot Pepper, we had to reconstruct the historical trajectory and character pose information to support moving reference systems and transition between them. The high frequency of animation shredding caused by motion matching in the context of a twitch shooter put high stress on the inertial blend we initially adopted. Inertializing continuously between non-necessarily similar poses was causing visible extrapolation defects, like ripples in trajectories of fast-moving limbs. So we introduced a simple blend stack implementation. Blend stack is an anim node in charge of playing animation asset until the blend weight fades away, implementing a proper dynamic interpolating blend tree used by motion matching during asset selection. We then use procedural behaviors to mitigate the discrepancies introduced by our doctoring between motion model and query as well as the discrepancies between the query and the selected animation caused by the lack of animation coverage. A mix of stride warping and play rate scaling were used to compensate differences between the capsule and the selected animation speeds. Blend profiles with faster blend speeds on the character lower body were used to mitigate feet sliding during blends. And orientation warping helped bending the selected animation towards the requested input trajectory. And for better results, we leverage blend stack graph support to perform orientation warping on every animation in the blend stack prior to blending them together, as you can see from the multiple green debug lines in the video. The final touches to the setup were made by tuning what we call Continuing Pose Bias, a cost discount for poses playing as continuation of the current animation that allowed us to reduce animation shredding by sticking with the currently-playing animation as much as possible. We also added block transition notify states to prevent jumping into section of animations that don't play just right unless played from the start, like drastic turns or jumps where you would never want to pick a pose after the character has left the ground. For specific assets like looping animation, we disable the selection of poses from the same asset, and we added a ringbuffer, keeping track of previously-selected poses to avoid repetition of past choices that would make it look less natural.
