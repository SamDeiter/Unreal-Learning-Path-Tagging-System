There are several important factors when choosing a video as the basis of our facial animation. In this section we'll break those down. There are several pros and cons for each device we may want to use to record our performance. Head mounted cameras offer very steady footage but are expensive. Stationary cameras are convenient and provide head rotations, but usually require additional tracking adjustments. The factors we'll cover in this module may help you determine which camera may best fit your needs. We want to maximize our pixel real estate while making sure that the actor's face remains within the frame. A buffer in all directions will guarantee that we don't lose parts of the face. If recording from a stationary camera like a webcam, actors may move their head but only to a certain extent. Ideally, we want to stay within a range of 10-30 degrees in any direction, otherwise we may cause certain facial features to get occluded, which will make the tracking process a bit trickier. The team at face wheel recommends working with 60 frames per second as the frame rate. Most consumer webcams are limited to 30 frames per second. However many recent cell phones and digital camcorders can support 60 and even up to 240 frames per second. A high frame rate means reduced motion blur and more precision between key facial expressions. That being said, a higher frame rate also means more data to work with, which usually means much heavier files. Short shadows and overexposure can cause an analyzer to lose certain facial features. The goal is to have a consistent and diffuse lighting to minimize shadows. A good focus will ensure the pixels stay clear and sharp, allowing the facial landmarks to latch on a lot better. Blurry footage is much harder to track. The duration of our video will directly translate to the number of frames of animation we're producing. Keep that in mind prior to recording or when you're planning out your sequence. In the next section, we'll talk about editing our videos to only work on the essentials. When working with audio and body motion for the same performance, we'll need to consider ways of syncing our data. A traditional clap or a more advanced timecode sync are available options. A facial ROM, short for range of motion, consists of asking our actors to hit a wide range of facial expressions in a short amount of time, which will allow us to build a robust tracking model and shared pose library. This can be very useful when working with several performances of the same actor applied to the same character. While facial markers are not directly evaluated by analyzer, it's useful for the user to position the facial landmarks without having to guess. This reduces potential human error and increases consistency between training frames.
