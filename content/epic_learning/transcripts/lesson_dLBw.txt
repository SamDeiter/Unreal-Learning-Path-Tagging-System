Every piece we add to our experiences has a cost. It can be paid for by a hardware CPU, its GPU, or the user's patience. In this module, we are going to discuss why performance matters. Each thread, the game thread, draw thread, and the GPU thread, each have their own jobs to handle to make sure our experiences run reliably. Our job is to make sure the tasks they are given are manageable. Broadly speaking, the game thread is all of the context of the app. It calculates what is where. After the game thread has finished its calculations, the draw thread will take that data and decide what to render. This will make sure that only what needs to be rendered for that frame is collected and it passes this information to the GPU. The GPU will take all of this data and create the final pixels that we see. Our target device needs to maintain this every frame at a minimum of 60 frames per second. When we take care to build to the target hardware's constraints, to maintain optimal frame rate and keeping the end users comfort at the front of our planning, it will set the stage for an effective user experience. It will also help manage the scalability of the project. The more performance our projects are, the more ready to go hardware will be able to support it. Keeping the assets maintained and well built will clearly allow new features or additions to be added. It also makes scaling the project much more enjoyable. When planning the project, budgets should be laid out to build from during development. A multi-level video field walkthrough app that requires large screens playing multimedia productions with flashing and moving lights around a main stage will have a very different budget than a mixed reality wavefinding app. It will also have very different target hardware. While these two examples are vastly different, both require the expectation that their target hardware will run these with as little issue as is possible. It is uncommon for a single issue to be the source of a poorly performance app. Much more common is the combination of all of our assets. Meshes, textures, materials, lighting, physics, media, blueprints, and more all have a cost. Quickly vetting the scenes can be as simple as asking for the total number of active assets there are in the scene and how complex are they. We can use tools that are ready to go inside of Unreal Engine to assist us in finding the problems. These steps are not only to hit a specific frames per second metric, but also to provide a comfortable experience to the end user so they can be engaged with the project. At the very beginning of the learning path, we discussed points to remember to ensure that we are providing a comfortable experience for that user. The last thing we want is the performance of our experience to cause sickness or discomfort. We will approach these issues by modifying our project settings to cater to our HoloLens 2 project requirements, matching very similar requirements of mobile devices. We'll rewrite the materials that came along with the import of our assets and take a look at the size of the textures used within. After removing content and maps that are unneeded, we'll clean up our blueprints. Then we will profile the final project to search for bottlenecks and finally debug a packaged version.
