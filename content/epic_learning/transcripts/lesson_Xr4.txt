We've covered several different topics as we went through the process of creating a realistic environment in Unreal Engine using DataSmith for Revit. First, we exported a DataSmith file from Revit and we imported that file into Unreal Engine. Using the DataSmith file format, we were able to bring in all of our lights, geometries, textures, materials, and metadata into an Unreal project. We then optimized that data using Python scripts and available metadata. We were able to replace materials across our entire project and we also identified other ways that you could use metadata to improve your workflow. We discussed master materials and material instances and created our own views in our project. Material Instancing can really help to streamline and unify how materials are created and used in your project. We imported a mountainous background into our project from the Landscape Mountains project available in the Epic Games Launcher and we imported several assets from the Epic Zen Garden project as well. We placed entourage assets in our scene such as furniture, foliage, and light fixtures. Once we had all the geometry that we needed, it was time to add lighting. We placed sphere reflection captures around our environment, then created a skylight and directional sunlight to light our environment. We discussed the differences between baked and dynamic lighting, added spotlights with IES profiles, and we even discussed several of the world settings options that are typically used for architectural visualization. Next, we built our lighting and reviewed some common issues that you may see from your lighting. Some of these issues, such as chunky shadows or light seams between objects, could simply be fixed by increasing the light map resolution on any problematic objects. After our light was built and we were satisfied with our model, it was time to add a post-process value to our project. With this, we were able to add subtle effects like bloom and lens flare and we were able to color grade our image to change the overall look of the environment. We also discussed ambient occlusion, global illumination, and how these will affect the look of our final render. We concluded this course by creating high res image captures and a video sequence using the sequencer tool. We added cinematic camera actors around our level to simulate actual real world cameras and animated them in a timeline. I would encourage you to continue improving your project and experimenting with some of the topics that we covered in this course. You could continue to add additional content from content packs or projects that you have already made. You could make changes and add detail in your refit model, but import those changes. You could add additional substances and materials, add baked lights with IES profiles, and create more still images and videos of your photo realistic environment to show off your work. This is Steve Began for Epic Games, hoping you have a great experience using Unreal Engine for your future architectural visualization projects. Thank you for participating in this course.
