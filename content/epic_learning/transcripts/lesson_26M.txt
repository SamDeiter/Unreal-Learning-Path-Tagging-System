Creating audio that dynamically changes based on the environment that it is in and the location of the user is a strong way to capture their attention, but it is not the only reason audio can make a huge difference in a project. In this module, we will discuss the differences in spatial audio and traditional stereo audio and wind to use each. We'll start with traditional audio as it is much more common on the devices we use in our day-to-day lives. So much so that we don't even think about it. Open or turn on an audio playing device. Be it a portable speaker, a phone, a tablet, a TV, or whatever else you have lying around, but not headphones. Turn on or press play. The audio that is now coming out of the speakers on the device is coming out at a volume of our choosing and it doesn't care where we are. If we look away from the source and close our eyes, we can still identify where it is in our world. We can, of course, leave the room, have it hidden from us, and still find it if it is playing audio. But this is not a mechanic built into the device. It doesn't care where you are. It is simply creating sound waves that are affected by the environment they're traveling in and eventually hitting our ear drums at their own time and intensities. It is, of course, our brain that is making the distinction of where the source of the sound is. The use of traditional stereo audio is important because it is expected to be attainable. It is intended for us to hear it at any time regardless of the situation. But if the user is wearing headphones and the sound is something happening across the room plays into the headphones at the same left and right intensity, there is no way for the user to discern the origin of the sound. But that may not always be important information to know. Traditional stereo audio is a great tool to take advantage of in our projects, even in a system as advanced as the HoloLens 2. Our projects can play specific sounds on UI to give feedback on a successful interaction or update. This can play during 2D or 3D guided step-by-step instructions or to convey a change with the experience. There are many examples of where fire and forget sounds are played and no longer need to be managed. Moving on to the other side, we can prepare our sound system to respond information about the environment that it has been placed in. Spatialized sound emulates how human hearing works allowing the user to identify the location of the source. Working inside of a 3D engine presents new problems that need solutions. The holograms we are building and interacting with have their own 3D world location and we are placing them in our reality using the HoloLens 2. For our project, the device we are using to help immerse the scene into our world does care where we are relative to where the source of our sounds are coming from. The speakers on the HoloLens can play a sound just as any other device, but the headtracking and audio system we will set up will alter the volume in each ear by where it is in the world relative to the listener. Using spatialized audio with a tenuation occlusion will take the environment and the listener's distance from the source into consideration. The meshes that obstruct the listener from the source can all be calculated to change what the user hears. Combining these two methods of adding audio to the scene inside of the experience being made, we can decide which audio should be placed in the world at a specific location for our focus and which should be played in traditional stereo to keep us updated. In this module, we discussed the differences between spatialized audio and traditional stereo audio and what each is best used for and the combination of the two that will assist us in creating an immersive reality experience while keeping our end user up to date.
