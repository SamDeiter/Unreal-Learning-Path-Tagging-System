When we are designing ways for our users to interact with objects or UI in the scene, we need to prepare for the easiest means to allow them to do so. We also need to create a system to prevent unwanted actions. If, for example, we allow a button to be pressed simply by looking at it, we may accidentally change values we did not want to change. Or if we wanted meshes to be destroyed if we look at them, we may end up destroying parts of our scene that shouldn't be. So a simple system could be that a user needs to stare at the intended target, the button or the mesh in this example, for four seconds consistently for it to interact. After the call for interaction, we can add in another check to determine exactly what we are interacting with and how we handle it. This is an interactive mechanic known as Gaze and Dwell. We look at something and wait for an amount of time until our intention is clear, then respond. On the other hand, we can create a system that waits for an action to be treated as an interaction. An example using only Gaze could be a UI button or mesh that is only pressed if the user stares at it and blinks twice. This is a simple example known as Gaze and Commit. Hollow Lens 2 is ready to be used for both and each have their examples where they work best and where another route may be beneficial. We will go over and create mechanics for both Gaze and Dwell as well as Gaze and Commit in the next couple of modules. If we were to try and really slowly move our eyes around for longer than a couple seconds, it becomes unnatural. We are much more used to constant and dynamic movements around our world. So interactions that don't require accuracy will work better with eye tracking. Identifying where a user is looking or what a user is looking at exactly would be a good use case for eye tracking. There are many projects that require the user to specifically look at what the developers want them to see. If they are perhaps allowing their gaze to drift away, we can build mechanics to attract their attention where it is needed. If I were to ask you to look only at one of these dark squares in the background on the wall here, it can get really annoying as there is no one thing to focus on. But if I ask you to keep your eyes on this red sphere, it becomes much easier. On the other side, our head movement can be remarkably steady and moved slowly. In this case, it would be jarring to expect a user to move their head quickly, let alone as quickly as our eyes can move. However, we need to keep in mind that while we think and feel that we are facing straight ahead of us, it is difficult to be accurate with our head gaze direction without a line or end point to show us our actual facing direction. Many of us rest our head at an angle in some direction without even thinking about it. So we have one part of our gaze that's chaotic, but with quick accuracy, and another that is steady but can be inaccurate. It will of course be the combination of the two of these that will provide us the best results. In this module, we demonstrated some differences in eye and head tracking and when to use them. As our learning path progresses, the combination of eye, head and hand tracking will allow us a fully natural system of interacting with our projects.
