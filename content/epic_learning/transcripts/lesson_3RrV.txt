So although this feature is still in beta in 427, the underlying technology is mature and already in production used today. So, first things first, what exactly is a container? At its simplest, a container is a mechanism for packaging up a piece of software with all of the components that it needs in order to run. Once it's been bundled up into a container image, the software is then portable, and easy to transfer between local systems or the cloud. In much the same way that a traditional shipping container makes it easier to transport physical goods. The benefit of this approach is that complex software, like the Unreal Engine, can be deployed across a wide variety of different environments and always provide a consistent experience, regardless of whether it's running on a local development workstation or on a massive compute cluster in the cloud. The lightweight way that containers are implemented at a technical level also means that you can pack a lot more of them into a system than technologies like virtual machines, which have traditionally been used for cloud deployments. The net result is that you can deploy Unreal Engine workloads faster and more cheaply than was previously possible, and you can also rapidly scale those deployments in response to user demand.
