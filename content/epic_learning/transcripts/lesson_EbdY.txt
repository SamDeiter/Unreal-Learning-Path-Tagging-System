This course is all about implementing music in your game and getting it to dynamically respond to game events and variables. We're going to assume that you have some familiarity with the engine and we'll be relying on several concepts that we've covered previously in the basic introductory courses on audio in Unreal Engine. Firstly, the course on ambient and procedural sound design and secondly the course on dynamic audio. So if you haven't already done these courses then now would be a good time. Throughout the course we'll be referring to systems that we've built in a demonstration game project. You'll be able to download the final version and this will include all the blueprints that we're discussing in the course so you can open these up and try them out with your own music as we go along. After looking briefly at how to implement simple looping music and music playlists we'll quickly progress to music that's triggered or influenced by events in the game. We're calling this dynamic music since it changes dynamically to respond to game events or variables. You might also hear other people refer to this as adaptive or interactive music. We'll start with looking at how to trigger simple musical one shots and then we'll focus initially on parallel music systems. This is where you compose music in parallel layers that can be mixed in or out. This approach is really good for representing game parameters and for responding quickly to changes in game states. We'll then look at how to implement transitional music. This is where you want to move from one music track to another. In some circumstances you'll want to do this since it can be more dramatic than the parallel approach but it does raise lots of challenges and implications for how we write music for games. The course will finish off by looking at stingers, short musical phrases that are played over the top of existing music, and then finally will cover some more advanced things you might want to try. All of these dynamic music approaches will be based around Unreal Engine's quartz system. This is highly accurate timing and it's built specifically for music and other audio based applications. By the end of the course you'll be able to implement a wide variety of dynamic music into your games. Before we start there's a couple of things to note about language. Although music is a universal language in terms of how it's generally written down and described in English speaking countries there are a couple of dialects we should be aware of. The approach from the United Kingdom dates back to when music originally started being written down and is Latin in origin. However the approach in the United States is a more recent invention based on the mathematical relationships between the notes. In these courses I'll always try to refer to both but I may occasionally slip up and default to the UK note names since that's my background in training. Similarly in the UK we tend to use the word bar to denote the period between bar lines whereas in the US the words bar and measure are often used interchangeably in this course we'll use the term bar.
