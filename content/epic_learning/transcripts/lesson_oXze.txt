When it comes to refining the lighting solutions that we are creating, the options found in Unreal's world settings tab, specifically inside the Lightmass rollout, can make a huge difference to the quality of our baked in direct lighting. And so, let's take a quick look in this video at some of the settings that can perhaps make a bigger difference than others. Firstly though, let's make sure that we are viewing the Lightmass settings inside of our UI, and then let's talk about lighting scale. Lighting scale essentially determines the samples used to create the global illumination. This is a setting, particularly if we are creating visualisations inside large open levels, that we may on occasion want to increase to a value of 2, in order to help keep performance at a reasonably high level. It can be lowered when working on smaller levels if we like, although I would recommend doing this with caution, as it can rapidly increase bake times if pushed just a bit too far. If you don't specifically need to add extra quality to a scene's global illumination, it is probably best to leave this setting alone. If we set a build scale of 0.9 for this particular scene though, given it's very small nature for a real time environment, we may help bring out some of the more complex details found in shaded areas of the scene, especially where we have more complex static mesh shapes casting shadows. Do be careful not to introduce unwanted noise to the environment though when using this setting. Let's also change our indirect light bounces to a value of 10, as this will help us move the indirect or bounce light around the scene in a much more realistic manner, given that in the real world light essentially just keeps on bouncing until it runs out of energy, which in a complete sense is pretty much never. More than 10 bounces in an unreal setting does tend to bring increasingly diminishing results, as the visual difference seen by the end user, in bounces above 10 that is, will be negligible to, well nonexistent. Careful testing can help us ensure that we are not being wasteful with our computing resources in this regard. We could also increase the lighting quality setting for finer detail here, although it should be noted that smoothing should always be lowered at the same time, otherwise we end up adding more detail with longer calculation times to the scene, and then smooth our average that out, bringing very little if any visual benefit to the scene. With that in mind then, let's set these options to 1.15 and 0.9 respectively then. If we come back to our build menu now, you will notice that we do have a fly out that lets us adjust the build quality of our lighting, so let's change that over to production quality. Although this will add quite a bit to our bake times at this point, and so if you were just wanting to quickly keep moving through the course, you may want to use a lower setting here in order to save time. Finally, let's rebake our lighting and see how, or if, those changes have improved the solution in any way. Which as you can see, it has. Being able to fine tune the quality of the light bake we are getting out of Unreal will obviously help us find the perfect blend of speed, versus quality for inner and all real-time visualization projects that we create.
