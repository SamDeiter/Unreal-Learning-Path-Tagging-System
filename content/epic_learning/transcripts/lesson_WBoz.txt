Implementing music for video games is certainly challenging, but hopefully the techniques we've described in this course and the blueprints provided in the demonstration level will be useful in getting you started. Whether real challenge lies is in composing the music itself and composing it in such a way that your conscious of how it will actually work when used dynamically. Often you'll find yourself composing music in your door that can feel quite static, knowing that the change, structure and drama in the music is actually going to come from its response to game events and variables. This non-linear approach to composing music is something that's new to many people. Traditional linear approaches to composition work fine in cutscenes or at moments in the game when you know the music won't be interrupted. But often during active gameplay, when the timings of events are indeterminate, this kind of approach doesn't work at all. Because you're just setting up musical expectations and structures that will inevitably get broken. The key to good game music is that it works with the design of the game. Working with game engines and dealing with systems like blueprints may be a world away from your compositional background, but by becoming familiar with these you'll be better able to be a participant in creating the game rather than someone who just hands over assets. We'd encourage you wherever possible to get involved in the game's development as early as possible so that your music and your ideas can be an integral part of the game's design.
