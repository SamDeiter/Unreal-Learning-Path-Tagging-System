Having let our interior with a directional light mimicking sunlight pouring in through the windows, I just want to take a small detail here and spend a bit of time looking at an important concept in connection with directional light shadows, when lighting large scale outdoor scenes. So maybe a house with extensive landscape gardens are an industrial complex spread over many acres. Whatever the actual setting though, large scale exteriors would probably be a situation in which we would want cascade shadows to come into play, as they can help us keep real-time performance high without sacrificing all of the shadow quality in a scene. To help demonstrate the feature, I am using the Abind his kite demo that can be downloaded from the Unreal Marketplace. And so, let's select our directional light and then press the F key to focus in on it. Again, the mobility setting that we choose will be important here. So static means that all light information will be baked to light maps, and so position color and intensity of the light cannot be changed at runtime. Stationery will cause only the shadow information to be baked, and so the light can change color and intensity at runtime. Whilst movable, will cause the light to become fully dynamic and so able to change, not only color and intensity at runtime, but also position as well. So let's say in this instance then that we want to have movable sunlight in the scene so that we can change the time of day inside the visualization. If we have a movable light, then our shadows will be dynamic, and dynamic shadows are computationally expensive. Because of this, using the cascade shadow option, basically the method of splitting our shadows into multiple layers of detail will definitely be able to help. And if we look down the list of options, we see that we have a cascade shadow map rollout. In the 3D view, let's get really close to the shadows that our trees are casting, so that we can see how our first option will work, which is quite a mouthful to say, in that it is called dynamic shadow distance movable light, which is currently set to a value of 20,000. This setting is measured from the player camera and is the maximum distance at which our shadows will still be drawn. If we had selected to use this as a stationary light, then the currently greyed out distance option would also be available and working just the same way. As we move the camera up into the sky, while still looking down at our shadows, we can see that they actually blend from sharp to less detailed to quite low quality, and if we go high enough, they disappear completely. If we increase our shadow distance setting to 40,000 though, we should see the shadows pop back into view. Let's increase the number of times our cascade shadow splits by setting the num dynamic shadow cascades to a value of 6. The distribution exponent control essentially determines where or how the cascades are distributed, so either close to or further away from the camera. Now as we increase the value, we see the shadows disappear more quickly, as the cascades are stacked closer to the camera. But as we lower it, the cascades are moved away from the camera and towards the max shadow distance like so. In fact, with this parameter set to a value of 1, we can clearly gauge individual cascade shadow quality by lowering the number of dynamic cascades to 1 whilst up close to the shadows, and then step all the way up to a value of 6. The ability to use and control cascade shadows on large scale outdoor visualisations can definitely help improve performance whilst keeping the overall look of the final visualisation at a very acceptable quality level.
