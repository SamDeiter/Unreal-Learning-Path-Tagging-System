With our landscape in place and painted up with some basic materials, time to move on to some of the more technical aspects of working with the terrain so that we can get the best out of it. We will be taking a look at creating collision meshes for terrains and then also at making some adjustments to them. Firstly though, let's talk a little bit about the difference between simple and complex collisions in connection with a landscape mesh. A mesh essentially being just a standard primitive shape, such as box, sphere or capsule, whereas a complex setup would use the trimesh of the terrain object itself. By default, one real engine creates both simple and complex shapes inside the physics engine and then, based on what it is that the user wants, complex query versus simple query, will use the corresponding shapes. To see the controls involved here, after selecting any landscape actor that has been created, we would go to the details panel under the collision section, where we will find two settings called collision-mip level and simple collision-mip level. What do these do? And how do they help us with collision? Well, the collision-mip level control really determines how complex the collision used for the landscape actually is, so in essence controlling what level of detail is used. Setting the collision-mip level to 0, the default, will give us very accurate landscape collision, though we should keep in mind that this will be at the expense of increased sometimes greatly increased memory usage. And so, a good rule of thumb to go by will be to save complex collisions for use on small scale projects that have a limited amount of terrain in them, or on projects where the terrain is a focus of the visualization and so would require accurate collisions to be present. Setting this to the max value of 5 will make landscape collision less memory intensive, but of course the accuracy of the collisions will suffer. As we have a controllable player in this project already set up, feel free to try both settings, and then walk on complex areas of the terrain in order to see the differences that this makes. Just as a word of warning, make sure that the player actor is above any terrain that we have sculpted before proceeding. Now the simple collision-mip level control also works in much the same way as the complex. In that setting it to 0, again the default, will give us a very accurate, albeit simpler landscape collision, but again at the expense of memory usage. Setting this to the max value of 5 by contrast will make landscape collision cheaper, but the accuracy of the collisions will of course suffer greatly. Now something that we may want to have set up on certain projects would be the ability to have more complex collision detection appear only in areas of the terrain where it will be needed. In other words, the ability to set the collision-mip level for individual landscape components. To be able to do this we will need, from the modes tab, to click on the landscape option, and then select the manage mode option if needed. If we then select a few landscape components by clicking on them with the selection tool, the selected landscape components become highlighted in a ready orange colour. Over in the details panel then we can expand the landscape components section if needed, and change both the collision and simple collision-mip levels to values of 2 and 3 respectively. Finally let's deselect any currently selected landscape components and select alternative ones. We should see the mip level values change inside our details tab. So being able to change collision mesh complexity, not only for the whole landscape, but also for individual sections, means that we can, hopefully, craft the best experience possible when it comes to navigating across our terrain, whilst at the same time controlling how much of an impact the terrain has on the overall performance of a level.
