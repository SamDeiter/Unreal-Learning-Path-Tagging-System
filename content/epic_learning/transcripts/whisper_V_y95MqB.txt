Hi folks, I'm here to talk a little bit about level design. Project Titan's world was built on a massive scale, and that's how you experience it in the editor. If this was a full game, most people would experience it from a player's eye view. That is, the camera, character and control systems driving our player character. What this means is we have to build with all those things in mind. When you work in editor, it's easier to get detached from a user. Remember, this is a huge world, but not everything in it needs to be huge, or nothing will have any impact. The texture and small details of a place here is important, if not more so, than any specific giant landmark. Details matter. As such, make sure to spend time actually experiencing your work from a player's eye view. Yes, it'll be rough around the edges and you'll have to use your imagination, but try and explore the world and you'll work within it from a player's perspective. How does it feel to come up a rise and see a city laid out before you? What catches your eye when you enter a space? What do you walk past or through? What do you see in the foreground, mid-ground, distance and horizon? Does the world feel tactile to you? Can you just about reach out and touch it? Does it feel good to navigate? Think about traversal. How does the player pass through the space? Is it interesting? Do you walk a straight line, run a curve or completely divert your path to reach the other side? Do you engage with movement mechanics or are you just walking? All of that matters. That said, moderation is as important as variety. If every bridge is broken, it becomes predictable. If you surprise a player, you'll engage and delight them too. Not all of these questions are answerable on your own. This is a collaborative project, so think about the team. Who can you work with to bring your work to life? To add those details? To make the world feel really alive? That said, when I say alive, I don't want you to think that this is about realism. Titan is a painterly stylized world with a ton of charm and personality. This can be incredibly useful from a level design as well as creative POV. The first advantage we have relates back to camera and control. Titan is a third-person experience and we need to make accommodations for camera and play control. Doors that technically are to scale with the character will tend to feel small and fiddly when you try and walk through them because we're controlling from a third-person POV, meaning we're some distance from the character. Rooms that are technically to scale will have the same problems, plus the addition of low ceilings to press the camera down and corners to trap it in. You can't really enjoy being in a room if looking around is frustrating. Building out spaces with the camera in mind will lead to a smoother experience. As we are working in a stylized aesthetic, we can more easily make oversized or wide-angled doors and high ceilings work within our environments. Secondly, there is affordance. That is, recognizability. In games, we often use this term to mean things should look like what they do, but given that Titan is primarily an art-driven experience, it's more about, what does this silhouette read as from a distance? So your beautiful work doesn't dissolve into a mass of unreadable collies on the horizon. Creating readable shapes means that even as you lose detail, your work remains recognizable. Being stylized means that you aren't bound by conventional forms, so you're free to experiment with shape. Just make sure it fits into the larger world too. Remember, folks, sense of place. Finally, I want to talk about collision. Each object and project Titan can have a collision mesh, which is normally a simplified representation of the object's shape. Objects can have simple or low-resolution collision, complex collision, or both, with different collision traces against different collision types. For example, often stairs use a ramp as its simple collision, enabling smoother player and camera movement, but have more detailed complex collision to support character IK. You can even use one to fill in for the other. Although using complex for simple collision processes can be inefficient, especially at Titan's scale. Collision can be generated or imported. For simple meshes, generated may be enough, but often collision is the smarter solution for anything of scale. You can simply model collision alongside the mesh in the modeling tool of your choice, following the crate naming conventions for collision assets, and import it alongside your mesh into Unreal. Make sure to double-check your import settings. For more, see the Unreal Engine 5 Static Mesh Pipeline documentation. I'll throw a link in the readme channel. This counts for meshes you've already modeled. Just go back into your modeling tool, set up collision, and re-import from the source. You can also author collision directly in Unreal's modeling mode using the Mesh to Collision tool. If you've imported or are planning to import a mesh into Titan, especially if it's a large one, put some thought into authoring collision so you don't block huge sections of the world. Collision is also more than just a thing can or cannot go here. The Unreal Engine Collision system allows for a wide range of collision traces for everything from player and camera to static environmental objects. In Titan, we even have some custom categories. These can be set using presets and overwritten, or set entirely to custom. You don't need to go beyond presets in most cases, but it's worth thinking about what category your asset fits into. When building elements for level design, you need to think about collision from a player's perspective. There's nothing more frustrating than getting hung up on small bits of collision as you explore a space. Process should start with, does this even need collision? Many open worlds make grass, shrubs, and even small trees collision-free. That means you can also call them as low levels of detail. If a thing does need collision, how complex does that collision need to be? See the Titan Tech Art Guide for more details there. Finally, consider how collision can help a player or guide the camera. Maybe your door's collision is subtly angled to guide a player through the frame. Maybe your roof has some invisible camera collision to keep the camera from getting caught up in the rafters of your ceiling. And those are some level design thoughts for today. Remember, think about the details, think about the space, think about the player experience moving through the space. Think about the character, camera, and controls that allow them to do so, and think about the collision that they're interacting with. And that's it for today on Level Design. Thanks for watching.
