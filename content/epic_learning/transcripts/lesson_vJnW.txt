Coming back to the scene in which we earlier set up our directional light, time to fill in some of the darker areas now with the aid of a skylight, which does pretty much what the name says, in that it mimics the hemisphere of light that we get from the sky as light from the sun is scattered in the Earth's atmosphere. This, in direct form of light, is sometimes called ambient light. Now how do we create such a light in Unreal? Well, from the light section of the modes tab, let's click and drag to add a skylight to the scene. In the world outline, just to keep things organized, let's add this to our light's folder. And then, if we come to the world settings tab, you will notice that as well as being able to control our indirect light bounces, we can also, in versions of Unreal since 4.19, control our skylight bounces as well. Let's set this to a value of 10 in order to get the maximum amount of light coverage for this particular scene. Again, just as with the directional light, anything above a setting of 10 bounces will start to give us rapidly diminishing returns. Indeed, 10 bounces may well be overkill for your particular scene, and so I would strongly recommend increasing this value in small amounts and then testing and comparing the results as you go, in order to get the best build time versus seeing quality balance that you can. Now as we come back to our details tab though, you may have noticed that we have mobility settings for our skylight just as we have with the other lights. The question we may want answering at this point then is does it work in the same way as with the point, spot and directional lights? Well, in simple terms, the answer is yes, but it will be good just to discuss what each of the mobility settings does here in order to have a nice clear picture once again. Setting the light to static, for instance, will bake the skylights lighting information to light maps, which, just as with the other lights, has the advantage of zero casting terms of performance at runtime, with the potential downsides being that all objects in the scene would need to remain stationary along with the light, and that the ambient shadows would be baked into the light maps as well, which in our case is actually fine, and so we will use the static option as we move forward. The stationary setting bakes only shadow information to the light maps, meaning scene objects must remain where they were placed at the time of the initial light bake. However, because only the shadows are baked, we can change the intensity color and even the cube map being used for our skylight at runtime if we want to. Now it should be noted here that any dynamic or movable lights in the level will not be captured and used for skylighting, whilst a static or stationary lights will be. Finally, we have the movable option, which when the source type option is set to the scene, and it is by default, this will use any light mobility type it can. This obviously is the most expensive in terms of performance cost at runtime. Let's set our skylight mobility to static then, so that it is ready for use in our next lesson.
