Hi folks, Sam here to talk about my process for getting to a good layout. I've worked across a lot of genres from FPS to RTS to all kinds of VR weirdness, and it almost always goes something like this. You start with ideation, which is a slightly annoying word but means the formation of ideas and concepts. Basically, before I dig into the actual layout I need to ask myself a bunch of questions that boils down to, what is the purpose of this layout? And by that I mean, what are the overall goals of this game or experience? And for Titan that'd be the brief up on ArtStation, stylized, painfully, third person exploration, and with that in mind, how will my intended layout reflect these goals? However, I also need to consider, what are my goals for the layout? How do I want it to play? How do I want it to feel? I need to reflect the larger goals of the game, but ultimately my own creative process is going to drive a lot of the day to day decision making, so I need to make sure I have something to work with. Next up, are my personal goals compatible with the wider goals of the project? If not, the project goals win by default and I rethink things a bit, because ultimately it's not about me, it's about the project. You may even want to do some initial noodling with your toolset. See what inspiration strikes, I keep a notepad nearby for sketching out ideas and rough concepts before I even open the editor. Once I'm reasonably happy with what I've got, I move on to the gameplay blockout. This can be done in the Unreal Editor with modeling tools or kitbash meshes, an external tool, whatever works best. The goal is to build fast, iterate a bunch, and then throw it out. These days I start with cube grid, where I set up a grid with the cell size roughly matching the size of the player pawn or some fraction thereof and build it up into a playspace. The gameplay blockout is a deeply iterative process, which in turn means it needs working collision and to be playable. Yes, you should be playtesting at this point. Spin up a test map or level instance, set the game mode and go from there. If you're working on a large open world game like Titan, it helps to reference the surrounding environment too. Main thing is that this is done fast, cheap and ugly. None of this geometry should survive past this point, it's just for stubbing stuff out. That's why I get twitchy when I see giant boxes littering the landscape in Titan, at least at this point in the project. Let's do it quick, don't spend forever noodling it, and move on. From here we start our visual blockout. Alright, here it's time to get real. Cubes have to go away and become actual representative meshes. If you can source them from content already created for the project, all the better. This point your overall space, scale and scope should be defined, now it's about filling in the details. Your shape language, sight lines, traversal, basic lighting and yes, representative collision, because you should still be playtesting. Never stop playtesting. You'll luckily find things you need to change, door and prop positions to improve traversal and sight lines, shifting elements around to better work with the shape language or lighting, creating more floor space in areas that require being more prop heavy and so on. That said, if your core layout doesn't work very well once you start adding actual art, then it wasn't a successful blockout. Go back to the previous step and iterate a bit more. I do this all the time, no point in sourcing assets if the layout isn't ready. By the end of the phase, I should know exactly what the space looks like and feels like. Once I'm happy here, and this will likely involve review by other disciplines, art, lighting, animation and so on, followed by iteration to make sure it aligns with their goals too, we go out of the blockout phase and start working towards a final set of assets for our layout. In other words, we are pushing towards final. I like to think of final as coming in two stages, Beta, which means you think it's complete, and Final, which means it's actually complete because we think it's complete too. So what does final mean? It boils down to all your assets being production ready and shippable, which means they meet the asset standards for the project in terms of mesh, material and collision, render correctly across all platforms and are something you're comfortable with putting out into the world. Don't overwork it, just get it right and move on. And that's it from me today. Thanks for watching and don't forget to playtest.
