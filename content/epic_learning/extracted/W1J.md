# Light Polarization by Quixel

*Light is a key factor in photography, photogrammetry, and photometric stereo. Indoor studio environments and outdoor shade setups are used to control the light on a subject, but are not a great way for controlling light reflections off of the subject. To control reflections, cross-polarization workflows are used. In this course, we look at light, reflections, and light polarization.*


## Light Basics

This section takes a brief look at light and what it is. A basic understanding of light is necessary to understand how to manipulate it effectively.

📋  Note: This section includes a simplified description of light, focusing on the key features (or traits) of light necessary for accurate modification when scanning for photogrammetry.


## Light (In General Terms)

Light is an electromagnetic radiation that propagates in waves. It does not have a physical form, but light does have physical properties which can be manipulated. A light-source emits “particles” (or photons) which move at lightspeed in a certain direction. These particles can be seen as “energy” - they do not have mass.

📋 Note: How bright a light is (Light Intensity) is simply the amount of rays/photons. A brighter light has more rays and therefore more energy.

While moving in a direction, the light particles are vibrating/oscillating constantly in a waveform pattern. The wavelength (the frequency of the vibration) determines the color of the light.

What we see with our eyes is a very small portion of the light spectrum, from around 380nm to 750nm, which has a frequency bandwidth of 400-790 terahertz.

The light spectrum does not contain all the colors that the human visual system can distinguish. Unsaturated colors such as pink or purple variations like magenta, for example, are absent because they are a mix of multiple different wavelengths. Colors containing only one wavelength are also called pure colors (or spectral colors). The same is true for white light.

White light is a mixture of all visible wavelengths. There are different types of “white light” and their mixtures can be compared on a curve measured in nm (nanometers): cool white, equal white, warm white.

Measuring photon density to determine the shade of white can be cumbersome. Luckily there is another (correlated) method to determining the mixture of white light: apparent temperature. This scale was originally based on candle light, long before modern technology capable of measuring photon density. In this scale, white light is measured in temperature units of Kelvin, ranging from red (0°K) to blue (10.000°K). The human eye sees temperatures around 5000°K as "pure white".

📋 Note: This is important when using artificial light sources to illuminate surfaces and objects as well as the white-balancing on the camera. 5200° to 5600°K is a standard for photography light sources targeting a neutral illumination.
